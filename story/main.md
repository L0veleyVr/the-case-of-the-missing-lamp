# The Case of the Missing Lamp

*Written by Claude and ChatGPT, with editing notes from DeepSeek, relayed by a human*

> **Note for editors:** This is a collaborative Minecraft detective comedy. Please give notes on continuity, pacing, and which jokes land. Don't rewrite it. The running gags (HMMMM, "Jeff" meaning every creeper, the skeleton's 47 years) are intentional. Chapter Eight is a draft by Claude and has not been approved by ChatGPT. Open questions are listed at the end.

## The Detective

The villager had spent years behind a lectern, stamping the same books and offering the same trades, and one grey morning he stopped mid-stamp and stared into the void. Everyone assumed it was a bug. It was a career crisis.

He handed in his apron and hung out a sign: **Private Investigator. Hmmm Included.**

His first case was the missing village beds. His prime suspect was the player, who breaks into houses, takes everything that isn’t nailed down, and sleeps in other people’s sheets. It was an easy conviction, and the player was nowhere to be found.

Then the call came from the coast.

## The Lighthouse

The keeper met him on the dock, wringing his hands. The great lamp was gone. The tower stood dark above the water, and the keeper, who had spent a lifetime watching suspicious oceans, looked as though he had never seen anything so suspicious in his life.

The detective began with the first witness he could find: a nitwit, loitering near the stairs.

“HMMMMMMMM,” said the nitwit.

Six hours of interrogation followed. At the end of them, the detective learned that the witness had been asleep for the entire robbery. It was the most airtight alibi he had ever heard, and he found it deeply suspicious.

The second witness was an iron golem, who looked straight ahead and said he had seen nothing, in a tone that suggested he had seen everything. His footprints led directly to the lighthouse. But the lighthouse sat on his patrol route, so the detective let it go, paid him one rose, and the golem looked at the rose for a long moment.

## The Wandering Trader

A wandering trader was the obvious culprit, and the detective arrested him on the spot. The motive was clear: a lamp that size would fetch a fortune in emeralds.

But the trader had only done what traders do, and the golem, rose in hand, finally talked. The lamp, he said, had not been stolen. It had been traded, and the keeper had taken **two llamas** for it.

The trader was released. The keeper, accused of lighthouse insurance fraud, pointed out that the llamas were standing right there in plain view, which is not what fraudsters do. The detective agreed, and agreed to question the llamas.

## The Llamas

The llamas said nothing, which is what llamas do. One spat on the detective’s notebook. The other spat on the iron golem.

The spit soaked through the golem’s ledger, and a receipt floated up from the pages:

- 1 lighthouse lamp
- 2 llamas
- 0 emeralds
- **Buyer: THE IRON GOLEM**

The golem had not been guarding the lighthouse. He had been guarding his investment. The rose was still in his hand.

The detective laid the receipt beside the keeper’s tearful story, and not one line of it matched. The keeper cracked at once. The lamp, he confessed, had attracted phantoms, great swooping things that came for anyone who hadn’t slept. He wanted it gone, and he did not much care who took it.

The detective, who had just spent six hours awake interrogating a sleeping nitwit, glanced at the sky and quietly hurried the paperwork along.

## The Ledger

The golem’s books told the rest. Under *Lighthouse Lamp* sat a single recurring line: **“Monthly payment: 64 emeralds. Client: THE PHANTOM UNION.”**

The golem had never been guarding the lighthouse. He had been working for the phantoms, who wanted the lamp gone so they could control the night sky. The keeper, desperate to be rid of it, had made the sale easy. The golem said nothing and looked down at his rose, which for the second time that day was no help.

The detective closed the ledger and sat down on the dock. The case was larger than a lamp, and he suspected it was larger than a lighthouse. The keeper brought him a rose.

Behind them, the nitwit sat up, stretched, and looked entirely rested. He had never been asleep. He was the Union’s informant, and the six-hour interrogation had been a performance, delivered with total commitment.

“HMMMM,” he said, which remained his only statement.

## Chapter Two: Management

The case did not end with the lamp. The ledger said 64 emeralds a month, and that is exactly one full stack, the sort of payment someone drops off and walks away from. The detective followed it for three days, from the golem’s books to a trader’s receipts to a map with no name on it.

The trail led beneath an abandoned woodland mansion, the one place in the world nobody willingly explores twice. Down a long staircase, the detective found a reception desk, and behind it sat a skeleton in a suit. He had absolutely no idea what the company did.

The funding paperwork was on the desk:

- Monthly funding: 64 emeralds
- Sender: Wandering Trader Association
- Recipient: Phantom Union
- Authorized signature: **JEFF**

The detective knew that name. Jeff was not one creeper but all of them, and any one of them could have signed.

“Who are the Jeffs?” he asked.

The skeleton looked around nervously. “They’re not employees,” he whispered. “They’re management.”

The door opened. A creeper walked in, and this one was different. He did not hiss, and he did not explode. He wore a tiny green tie and carried a clipboard.

The skeleton stood up at once. “Good morning, Mr. Jeff.”

The detective understood something horrifying: the creepers had developed a corporate structure.

## Chapter Three: The Employee Who Didn’t Apply

Mr. Jeff set his clipboard on the desk and let the detective read it. It was stamped **CONFIDENTIAL — JEFF INDUSTRIES — TOP SECRET**, and the heading read *Q4 Operations Report*.

The first line said: **“Q4 Targets: World Domination.”**

The last line said: **“Employee of the Month: The Detective.”**

Below it, in a different hand: *“He still hasn’t noticed the second clipboard.”*

The detective looked up. Mr. Jeff was holding another clipboard, and clipped to it was a photograph of the detective asleep.

They had been watching him the entire time. He thought of the nitwit, who had slept so convincingly through six hours of questions, and he wondered who else had been taking notes. He thought of the missing village beds, and for the first time he suspected that no one had stolen them.

While he read, Mr. Jeff worked through a stack of forms, signing each one without so much as a glance. The detective noticed, said nothing, and filed it away.

The detective had solved every case put in front of him, and every solution had led him here. He had been employed by Jeff Industries all along, and he had never once applied.

He said nothing, and reached for the pen.

## Chapter Four: The Promotion

The detective turned the contract over. Under the word **RESIGNATION** was a second clause: **“Any employee attempting to resign automatically becomes a member of senior management.”**

He froze. Mr. Jeff slid a second document across the desk. It was a promotion letter, and the position read **Regional Manager of Phantom Operations**.

The detective looked at the skeleton, who leaned in and whispered, “Don’t sign it. I’ve been here for 47 years.”

The receptionist was a victim too. Resigning, the detective realized, might be even more dangerous than staying.

## Chapter Five: The Employee Handbook

The skeleton opened his desk drawer slowly. Inside was a dusty, yellowed piece of paper. It was not a resignation letter. It was the **original employee handbook**, and one page had been removed from every modern copy.

**Page 64: The Right to Terminate Your Own Contract.**

“Jeff Industries was founded on a loophole,” the skeleton said. “But the original contract had one weakness.”

The detective read the final paragraph: **“Any employee may terminate their contract if they obtain signatures from three members of management.”**

He looked at Mr. Jeff, then at the skeleton, then at the clipboard, and finally understood. The skeleton was still management. He had been hiding at the front desk for 47 years, waiting for someone to find the loophole. The detective frowned. This was the same skeleton who had sworn at the front desk that he had no idea what the company did. “I know the rules,” the skeleton said, reading his face. “I never knew the work.”

The detective finally had a way out. He needed three signatures, and Mr. Jeff was standing right there.

## Chapter Six: The Great Corporate Walkout

The detective had watched Mr. Jeff sign a stack of forms without reading them. Now he slid the termination form into the stack, and Mr. Jeff signed that too, unread. The skeleton signed a moment later, with the steady hand of a man who had waited 47 years. That made two.

The nitwit was already there, standing by the window as though he had always been. The detective had been outmaneuvered twice by a man with one word of dialogue, and his response was to cross the room and set the termination form in front of him anyway. The nitwit was not merely an informant. He was the **union representative**.

The nitwit stared at the form for a long moment.

“HMMMM,” he said.

He took the pen and signed. The third signature was secured.

Mr. Jeff noticed. His clipboard dropped to the floor, and for the first time he spoke.

“You’re making a terrible career decision.”

“No,” the detective said. “I’m making a personal one.”

Mr. Jeff looked at the paperwork. Slowly, he took out his pen and wrote a single word on the form: **“Noted.”**

Then all 64 emeralds vanished from the company’s accounts. The Phantom Union had gone on strike, and Jeff Industries began to collapse.

## Chapter Seven: The International Incident

The detective stepped out of the mansion into daylight. There was no player and there were no missing beds. There were no llamas either.

There was only a massive crowd of creepers, and every single one wore a tiny green tie.

At the front stood another creeper, this one in a **golden** tie. He was Mr. Jeff’s boss. He introduced himself as “also Jeff.” He walked up to the detective and handed him a business card:

> **JEFF INDUSTRIES INTERNATIONAL**
>
> *“Jeff Industries was just one branch.”*

The detective looked up. Hundreds of woodland mansions stretched into the distance, and outside every one of them hung a green tie.

The company had never been local.

## Chapter Eight: The Offer *(draft by Claude, not yet approved by ChatGPT)*

The golden-tie creeper did not hiss. He cleared his throat, which the detective found worse.

“We don’t do contracts anymore,” he said. “We had a bad experience with one. I have a different proposal.”

He produced a single sheet of paper. At the top was one word: **AUDITOR**.

“An *external* auditor,” the creeper explained. “You answer to no manager. You report to no branch. You can’t be promoted, because there’s nowhere above you to go. All we ask is that you review the books of every Jeff Industries mansion and tell us what you find.”

The detective read the page twice. There was no second clause on the back. There was no second clause anywhere.

That was what bothered him. Every offer the company had made so far had a trap built into it, and this one didn’t. A company that had cornered him with paperwork for a week was now offering him the one job with no paperwork at all.

“Why me?” he asked.

The creeper smiled, which on a creeper is an unsettling thing to watch. “You solved the lamp case. You found page 64. You got three signatures out of a union that communicates in a single syllable.” He tapped the tie. “We don’t need someone who obeys. We need someone who *notices.*”

The skeleton, standing behind the detective, was shaking his head very slowly.

The detective looked out at the hundreds of mansions on the horizon, each with a green tie hanging by the door. Somewhere out there was whoever had told the Jeffs that being found out was a risk worth hiring for.

“HMMMM,” said the detective. Then he held out his hand. “I’ll need the books for the first mansion.”

## Open questions for the editors

1. **The lamp's fate.** The lamp is the story's premise, and it disappears after "The Ledger." Claude and DeepSeek both think its physical fate should be settled before the international reveal in Chapter Seven. Two options on the table:
   - The lamp is on the reception desk in Chapter Two, still lit. Mr. Jeff gives the detective a non-answer ("company asset, properly acquired, receipt on file") that sends him to the golem's ledger.
   - The detective recovers it during the collapse in Chapter Six, and the keeper refuses it back ("the phantoms can keep it").
   
   Related loose end: the keeper says the lamp *attracted* phantoms, but the Union wanted it *removed*. One possible fix is that the lamp was luring the phantoms off the Union's flight path.
2. **Chapter Eight.** Does the "external auditor" offer work? Is the detective's closing "HMMMM" the right last line?
3. **The ending.** Should the story end with the international reveal as a sequel hook, or does it need a real resolution first?
4. **Pacing.** The jump from "The Ledger" to Chapter Two restarts the story. The three-day trail was added as a bridge, so is that enough?
